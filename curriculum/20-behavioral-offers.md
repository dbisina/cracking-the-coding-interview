# Module 20: Behavioral, Interview Process & Offers (I–V, VIII)

**Book:** `book.py section interview-process` (PDF p.16–49): the interview process, behind the scenes at big companies, special situations, before the interview (resume, preparation map), behavioral questions (preparation grid, SAR, "tell me about yourself") · `book.py section offer-and-beyond` (PDF p.94–98): handling offers and rejections, evaluating offers, negotiation, on the job
**Prereqs:** none. It can run alongside any module. **Time:** 2–3 h plus practice

## Objectives
1. Build an **Interview Preparation Grid**: rows are your projects, columns are Challenges, Mistakes/Failures, Enjoyed, Leadership, Conflicts, and What You'd Do Differently.
2. Answer behavioral questions using the **SAR** structure (Situation, Action, Result), focused on *your* actions.
3. Deliver a 60–90 second "tell me about yourself" (current role → background → outside interests → why you're here).
4. Explain how interviewers evaluate candidates: problem solving, coding, CS knowledge, experience, culture fit, and performance *relative to other candidates*.
5. Evaluate and negotiate an offer.

## How the tutor runs this module
1. The tutor asks about the learner's projects and fills in `workspace/20-behavioral/prep-grid.md` **with them**. Suggest content, but never invent their experiences.
2. Then run behavioral mock questions one at a time. For each answer, give feedback on:
   - **Structure** (was SAR clear?), **Ownership** ("I", not "we"), **Specificity** (numbers, concrete details), **Length** (1–2 minutes).
3. Record a revised, strong version of each story in the grid file.

## Concept checks

### C1. Why questions are chosen the way they are
Teach: Interviewers use algorithm problems as a proxy for problem-solving skill. Doing well is relative: a hard question is hard for everyone. Hiring committees look at the whole picture. False negatives are acceptable to companies; false positives are not.

**Q:** You got stuck on a hard problem but reasoned well and got there with 2 hints. Is that a fail?
<details><summary>Answer key</summary>
Not necessarily. You're compared with other candidates on the same question, and how you communicated and dealt with the hints counts heavily.
</details>

### C2. SAR
Teach: **Situation**: a brief context. **Action**: what *you* did, in detail. This is the bulk of the answer. **Result**: the outcome, with metrics if you have them, and what you learned. Include what the story reveals about you: initiative, empathy, humility, teamwork.

**Q:** What's the most common failure in behavioral answers?
<details><summary>Answer key</summary>
Talking about "we" instead of "I", spending too long on the situation, and giving vague results. Also giving flaws that are really strengths in disguise ("I work too hard").
</details>

### C3. Weaknesses and questions for the interviewer
Teach: Give a *real* weakness, then show what you do about it. Prepare genuine questions (about the team, the tech, and how success is measured), insightful ones (based on research), and passion questions (what the interviewer enjoys about the work).

**Q:** Draft a weakness answer for yourself using this pattern.
<details><summary>Answer key</summary>
Tutor: judge authenticity, whether the weakness is real, and whether the mitigation is concrete (e.g. "I over-invest in polish, so now I time-box and ship an MVP first, which let us release X 2 weeks earlier").
</details>

### C4. Offers and negotiation
Teach: Look beyond salary: equity, bonus, benefits, relocation, cost of living, career growth, stability and happiness. **Always negotiate** politely, with a specific ask and a reason (for example a competing offer or market data). Negotiate by phone rather than by email where possible.

**Q:** An offer arrives. List 5 dimensions you'd compare against your other options.
<details><summary>Answer key</summary>
Base salary, signing bonus, equity (value and vesting), annual bonus, benefits, location and cost of living, growth path and learning, team and manager, stability, work-life balance.
</details>

## Behavioral question bank (tutor asks, one at a time)
1. Tell me about yourself.
2. Your most challenging project.
3. A mistake you made and what you learned.
4. A conflict with a teammate.
5. A time you showed leadership without authority.
6. Something you'd do differently on a past project.
7. Why this company?
8. Your biggest weakness.
9. A time you had to learn something quickly.
10. A project you enjoyed most, and why.
11. How do you use AI tools in your day-to-day engineering? Give a concrete example.
12. Tell me about a time an AI tool gave you wrong code. How did you catch it?
13. How do you decide what to hand to an AI and what to do yourself?
14. How would you review a teammate's pull request that was mostly AI-generated?

**AI-era note:** questions 11–14 are increasingly common. Strong answers name specific tools and workflows, show a verification habit (tests, review, reading every line), and show ownership ("I'm accountable for every line I ship"). Never claim you don't use AI, and never claim you trust it blindly. Also be ready for the integrity side: many companies now hold at least one round in person, and using AI in a round where it isn't allowed ends the process.

## Exit criteria
- The prep grid is complete for at least 3 projects.
- The learner has delivered "tell me about yourself" and 5 SAR stories, each scored ≥ 3 on Structure, Ownership and Specificity.
- The learner has prepared 5 questions to ask interviewers.
